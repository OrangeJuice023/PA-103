# Balassa (1961): The Story Version

*Full note: [balassa_economic_integration.md](../balassa_economic_integration.md). The town story is an analogy for studying; the five levels and key ideas come from the reviewer PDF.*

## The one idea
**The deeper countries integrate their economies, the more they have to decide together, so the more they need shared governance.**

## The story: five towns and their toll gates

Imagine a group of neighboring towns. Each has toll gates on the road that taxes goods coming in. Balassa asks: what happens, step by step, if they start removing the gates?

First, a word: Balassa uses "discrimination" to mean **treating other countries differently** (different tariffs, rules, access). It is not the social meaning. Integration means choosing to remove some of that difference with chosen partners.

And a warning: **cooperation is not integration.** Talking about trade policy is cooperation. Actually *removing* a barrier is integration. The test is which barrier is actually gone, not how often they meet.

### Level 1: Free Trade Area
The towns remove the gates **between themselves**. But each still keeps its **own** gate against outsiders.

*Problem:* Town A taxes outside goods lightly, Town B heavily. Outsiders ship through Town A, then move freely into Town B (a back door). The fix: **rules of origin**, which decide what counts as "made here". (Example: USMCA.)

### Level 2: Customs Union
Now they agree on **one shared outside gate**, a common external tariff. No more back door, so no rules of origin needed. (Example: the EU customs union.)

The catch: someone has to decide the shared tariff. Each town gave up some say. **Governance starts here.**

Also, two effects (Viner):
- **Trade creation:** buying from a cheaper member instead of a costlier local producer. Good for efficiency.
- **Trade diversion:** buying from a costlier member because the cheaper outsider now faces the shared gate. A distortion.
So do not say "integration is always good because trade goes up".

### Level 3: Common Market
Not just goods now: **workers and money can move too**. The question changes from "can I sell there?" to "can I work, invest, or open a business there?" (Example: the EU Single Market.)

### Level 4: Economic Union
Countries **harmonize their economic policies**. Why? Once economies are tied together, Town A's tax policy hits Town B. Policy is no longer purely national. (The EU does a lot of this but is not a perfect match to Balassa's ideal.)

### Level 5: Total Integration
One unified monetary, fiscal, social, and countercyclical policy, with a **supranational authority** whose decisions bind everyone. **No real organization is here.** It is a theoretical endpoint.

Each level keeps everything from the one below and adds one thing:

| Level | What it adds |
| --- | --- |
| Free Trade Area | No internal tariffs |
| Customs Union | + common outside tariff |
| Common Market | + free movement of labour and capital |
| Economic Union | + harmonized policies |
| Total Integration | + unified policy and a binding authority |

## Where does ASEAN fit?
This is the thinking move the reviewer says matters most. ASEAN is **deeper** in some ways (goods, services, investment, capital, some professions through Mutual Recognition Arrangements) and **shallower** in others (no common external tariff, no full free movement of workers, no policy unification).

So "Is ASEAN integrated or not?" is the wrong question. Integration is **a set of dimensions, each with its own depth**. Use Balassa as a **measuring device**, not a checklist everyone must follow.

## The common currency question
Sharing a currency is not an automatic next step on the ladder. It extends into monetary integration.
- **You gain:** no money conversion, less exchange-rate uncertainty, easier price comparison, simpler transactions.
- **You give up:** your own monetary policy. If Country A is in recession and Country B has inflation, they still share one policy. That is an **asymmetric shock**.

In the euro area, the ECB runs one monetary policy while fiscal decisions stay national. That causes tension because different dimensions deepen at different speeds. This **tests** Balassa's framework; it does not disprove it.

## The punchline
Economic integration looks like it is about things: tariffs, goods, money. But the choices behind them are about **people and governments**: who can work where, what rules apply, who makes the rules, who can object. So the governance question starts **early**, not only at the last stage. Each time a government says "this is no longer only our decision", it has created a governance problem.

## Remember this
1. Integration is a **process and a state**.
2. Cooperation lessens discrimination; integration **abolishes** it.
3. Five levels: **FTA, Customs Union, Common Market, Economic Union, Total Integration**.
4. ASEAN is **not one box**.
5. Deeper integration means more shared decisions and more governance.

## Don't confuse
- *Free trade area vs customs union:* only the customs union has a **common external tariff**.
- *Common market vs economic union:* common market frees movement of factors; economic union adds **policy harmonization**.
- *Trade creation vs diversion:* from a costlier producer to a cheaper member vs from a cheaper outsider to a costlier member.
