# Haas (1970): The Story Version

*Full note: [haas_pretheorizing.md](../haas_pretheorizing.md). Analogies below are for studying; the claims come from the class slides.*

## The story

After World War II, Europe's countries started joining up, **voluntarily**, no force involved. Social scientists got excited: how does a bunch of proud, sovereign countries end up "mingling and merging"? And will it work in other parts of the world too?

For 15 years (1955-1970), researchers studied this. Haas stops and asks: **so, what did we actually learn?**

### Part 1: A big pile of facts that don't connect
They found 13 solid facts. Examples: common markets push integration the most; if people feel the deal is fair, interdependence feels good; in Africa, integration was mostly symbolic; outside powers do unpredictable things.

Think of a drawer full of puzzle pieces **from different puzzles**. Each piece is real. None of them fits together, and no one ranked which matters most. Haas calls it a "mixed bag".

### Part 2: Three teams, three theories (but really "pre"-theories)
Three groups of researchers each had their own idea of how integration happens. Haas says they are not full theories yet, only "pretheories":

| Team | What they believe | Everyday version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Federalists** | Build the right institutions and constitution and the region will unite | "Draw up the rules of the house first" |
| **Communications** | The more countries trade, write, and visit each other, the more they trust each other, until war becomes unthinkable (a **security community**) | "Friends who talk all the time stop fighting" |
| **Neo-functionalists** | Cooperate on one thing, and that creates new problems that push you to cooperate on more (**spillover**), all out of self-interest | "We shared a wifi bill, now we share the whole house" |

Each team also **picked its own finish line before looking at the data**. That is the trouble.

### Part 3: The real problem, nobody agrees what "success" is
Imagine three coaches judging a race, but each has a different finish line. Federalists say the finish is a federal union (like the US or Switzerland). Communications people say it's a security community. Neo-functionalists say it's a political community with central institutions.

Haas's point: since there is no **shared finish line** (a *dependent variable*), the findings can't be added up. They were never measuring the same thing.

Two earlier fixes, both with problems:
1. **Scales:** use several measuring scales instead of one endpoint. But people disagree on what goes on the scale, and it assumes "higher is always better".
2. **Lindberg and Scheingold's three outcomes:** for any task, did it get **fulfilled**, did it **retract** (go backwards), or **extend** (spread into new areas)?

### Part 4: Haas's own fix
He admits his fix is partly imaginary, but offers it:
- **Three "imaginary" finish lines:** a **regional state** (one center in charge), a **regional commune** (no center, power spread out), or an **asymmetrical overlap** (several uneven centers).
- **A measuring stick:** has **authority** moved from national to regional bodies, and do people see that authority as **legitimate**?
- **A three-step check:** (1) look at one sector, (2) add up all sectors, (3) judge the whole region.

Example: in the EEC, agriculture moved a lot of power to the region, energy moved very little. The uneven result looks like **asymmetrical overlap**.

Haas's own warning: these endpoints "have no real-life counterparts", so you still need human judgment. The slides ask: is this useful, or a fancy way of admitting defeat?

### Part 5: What he still can't answer
- Do the forces that **start** integration also **keep it going**?
- Do national and regional politics have to move together? (Haas: no. The EEC integrated economically while national politics got more nationalist.)
- Does the developing world need a different model? (Haas: same variables, different weights.)
- How do people **learn**? His biggest black box.

He proposes computer simulations to force people to state their assumptions, and ends with a speculation about whether a fragmented world or strong regional blocs is better for peace. The slides ask if that leap is analysis or just opinion.

## Remember this
1. Haas is a **critique and a pivot**, not a victory lap: "we have lots of facts and no real theory yet".
2. Integration = a **process** where states voluntarily give up some sovereignty and form a political community.
3. Three **pretheories**: federalist, communications, neo-functionalist.
4. Core problem: **no agreed dependent variable**.
5. His proposal: three terminal conditions plus authority-legitimacy transfer.

## Don't confuse
- *Pretheory* is not a failed theory. It names variables and methods before a theory exists.
- *Empirical generalization* is a true finding that is not part of a bigger theory.
- *Dependent variable* is the outcome you are trying to explain. Here it is "what is successful integration?".
