# Reading 11 — Deutsch, Karl W., et al. (1957)

**Citation (WEB, accessed 2026-10-27):**
Deutsch, Karl W., et al. 1957. *Political Community and the North Atlantic Area: International Organization in the Light of Historical Experience.* Princeton: Princeton University Press.

**Status:** WEB — outside research, not from class slides. Do not present as professor content.

**1. What is "integration"?**
A process of building a "security community": a group of people that has become integrated, with a "sense of community" and institutions strong enough to assure dependable expectations of "peaceful change" (resolution of social problems without large-scale physical force). Integration can be "amalgamated" (formal merger into one unit) or "pluralistic" (separate governments retain legal independence but form a security community).

**2. What drives it?**
Shared values, extensive and sustained communication and transactions between politically relevant strata, and a core political area around which community can coalesce. Integration is a learning process over a long period of contact. Deutsch and colleagues were skeptical of functionalism's claim that integrating government tasks one step at a time leads to successful amalgamation, though they confirmed functionalism had succeeded in the past.

**3. What are the actors?**
Populations, elites, governments, and political communities. The focus is on people and their "we-feeling," not just institutions.

**4. How is it measured or tested?**
Through comparative historical case analysis: ten cases of integration (Germany, Habsburg Empire, Italy, Norway-Sweden, Switzerland, UK, US) tested for whether they became security communities. Key variables: sense of community, communication/transaction flows, administrative capability.

**5. Builds on / argues against?**
Builds on community/communication theory. Argues against pure functionalism (Mitrany): functional task integration alone is insufficient without a sense of community. Also contrasts with Haas's neo-functionalism: Deutsch emphasizes community and transactions, not spillover from sectoral integration. Later revived by scholars interested in EU enlargement and European identity.

**Cluster:** Transactionalism.
