## One-sentence answer

The deeper the integration, the wider the scope of shared commitments, and the greater the need for shared governance. [p.1]

Balassa's question is not "what is an FTA" but "what changes when borders weaken", and for PA 103, "what happens to governance as economic integration deepens". [p.1]

## Who he is

Hungarian economist (1928-1991). Studied law in Budapest, left after the 1956 uprising, doctorate at Yale, professor at Johns Hopkins, World Bank consultant. Also known for revealed comparative advantage and the Balassa-Samuelson effect. A trade and development economist, so the 1961 book builds a systematic framework rather than describing one organization. [p.1]

## Five questions (North Star A3)

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| 1. What is integration? | **Both** a process (measures that abolish discrimination between economic units of different states) and a state of affairs (absence of that discrimination). [p.1] |
| 2. What drives it? | Removal of trade barriers, factor mobility, economies of scale, policy coordination, monetary integration (mechanisms to "explain"). [p.6] |
| 3. Who are the actors? | National governments that give up policy discretion, and eventually a supranational authority at the deepest level. [p.4] |
| 4. How is it measured? | The five-level ladder: ask which barriers have actually been removed and how deep, not yes or no. [p.5] |
| 5. Builds on / against | Viner on trade creation and diversion [p.3]; related to Mundell on optimum currency areas, 1961 [p.5]. Class links: Haas (how to theorise), Mitrany (functional cooperation), Etzioni (political communities). [p.6] |

## Key ideas

### "Discrimination" is not the social meaning
It means treating economic units of different states differently: different tariffs, market access, rules. Integration deliberately removes some of those differences between chosen partners. [p.2]

### Cooperation is not integration
- **Cooperation:** actions that *lessen* discrimination (e.g. "let us discuss our trade policies").
- **Integration:** measures that *suppress or abolish* particular forms of discrimination (e.g. "let us remove these barriers").

The test is which barrier was actually removed, not how often states talk. [p.2]

### The five levels
Each level keeps everything before it and adds one thing. [p.2]

| Level | Adds | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Free Trade Area | Internal tariffs and quotas removed; each member keeps its own external tariff | USMCA [p.3] |
| 2. Customs Union | Common external tariff | EU customs union [p.3] |
| 3. Common Market | Free movement of labour and capital | EU Single Market [p.3-4] |
| 4. Economic Union | Harmonised national economic policies | EU, though not a perfect match [p.4] |
| 5. Total Integration | Unified monetary, fiscal, social, countercyclical policy plus a binding supranational authority | None today, a theoretical endpoint [p.4] |

### Things worth knowing per level
- **Rules of origin (FTA):** without a common external tariff, goods could enter through the member with the lowest tariff and flow to the others (the "back door"). Rules of origin decide which goods qualify. A customs union does not need them. [p.3]
- **Governance point (customs union):** one external tariff means someone must decide it, so a member gives up discretion. [p.3]
- **Trade creation vs trade diversion (Viner):** creation shifts production from a costlier producer to a cheaper member (efficiency gain). Diversion shifts trade from a cheaper outsider to a costlier member because of preferential treatment. Do not say "integration is always good because trade increases". [p.3]
- **Common market:** the question changes from "can I sell there" to "can I work, invest, set up a business there". [p.4]
- **Economic union:** needed because one member's tax policy now affects another. [p.4]
- **Total integration:** governance questions become explicit: who makes rules, implements, monitors, settles disputes, and is accountable. [p.4]

### Does ASEAN fit the ladder?
The most important analytical move in the reviewer. [p.4-5]
- **Deeper:** goods, services, investment, capital, managed mobility of some professions via Mutual Recognition Arrangements.
- **Shallower or absent:** no common external tariff, no full free movement of workers, no policy unification.

So ASEAN does not fit one box. Use Balassa as a **measuring device**, not a roadmap everyone must follow. If asked "is ASEAN integrated or not", the question is malformed: integration is a set of dimensions with different depths. [p.5]

### Common currency
Not an automatic next step on the ladder; an extension into monetary integration. [p.5]
- **Gains:** no conversion between members, less exchange-rate uncertainty, price transparency, deeper integration. [p.5]
- **Cost:** you share decision-making, not only a medium of exchange. Asymmetric shocks (one member in recession, another with inflation, one monetary policy) must be absorbed by labour mobility, fiscal policy or financial mechanisms. [p.5]
- **Trade-off:** more monetary integration for less independent monetary and exchange-rate policy. The useful question is how much national policy autonomy governments will give up. [p.6]
- **Euro area:** the ECB runs one monetary policy while fiscal decisions stay national, which causes institutional tension because dimensions deepen at different speeds. This tests Balassa's framework; it does not refute it. [p.6]

### Describe, explain, predict [p.6]
- Describe: what level are we looking at?
- Explain: which mechanisms produce the effects?
- Predict: if integration deepens, which new problems appear (spillovers, coordination, adjustment, fiscal and monetary, institutional)?

### Closing question: when does economics become governance?
Not only at the last stage. Every time a government says "this will no longer be only our decision", it creates a governance problem. The "administration of things" slowly becomes governance of people because tariffs, labour, capital and currency are the choices of people and governments. [p.6-8]
