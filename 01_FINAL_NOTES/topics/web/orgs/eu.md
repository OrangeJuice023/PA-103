## 1. What it is
A political and economic union of European states, unique among regional organizations for its depth of supranational authority (the European Commission, European Parliament, Court of Justice of the EU, European Central Bank) and its single market and (for most members) the euro currency.

## 2. Founding
- Roots: 1951 European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC, Treaty of Paris, 6 members).
- 1957 Treaty of Rome established the European Economic Community (EEC) and Euratom.
- Formalized as the European Union by the 1992 Maastricht Treaty (in force 1 November 1993), adding pillars beyond economics (foreign policy, justice/home affairs).
- Later treaties: Amsterdam, Nice, Lisbon (2009).

## 3. Members
27 member states (after the UK's withdrawal/Brexit, 2020). Founding six (1957): Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, Luxembourg, Netherlands. Successive enlargements brought in most of Western, then Central/Eastern Europe.

## 4. Purpose
Ever-closer union among European peoples: a single market with free movement of goods, services, capital, people; common trade/competition policy; economic and monetary union (euro); common foreign and security policy; cooperation in justice and home affairs; promote peace, stability, and prosperity in Europe (post-war reconciliation, especially Franco-German).

## 5. Relevance
The paradigmatic case of deep regional integration; the model other regional organizations emulate or react against; currently faces challenges (Brexit, rule-of-law disputes, migration, Eurozone crises, enlargement to the Western Balkans/Ukraine/Moldova).

## 6. Theory lens
**Neo-functionalism** (Haas, Schmitter, Nye — readings 12/22; Schmitter reading 8): spillover from sectoral integration (coal/steel → common market → monetary/political union). **Supranational governance** (Stone Sweet & Sandholtz, reading 14): independent supranational institutions (Commission, Court) drive integration beyond intergovernmental bargains.
