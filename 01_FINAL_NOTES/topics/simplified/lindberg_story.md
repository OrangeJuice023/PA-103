# Lindberg (1970): The Story Version

*Full note: [lindberg_multidimensional.md](../lindberg_multidimensional.md). The check-up story is an analogy for studying; the ten properties and cases come from the class study guide.*

## The one idea
Political integration is **not one thing you can score out of 10**. It is a **collective decision-making system** with many parts that can move up, down, or stay still independently. So measure many things first, then explain.

## The story: a medical check-up for a region

When you go for a check-up, the doctor does **not** give you one number called "health". They check blood pressure, heart rate, cholesterol, and so on. One part can be fine while another is bad. Adding all the numbers into one score would hide the problem.

Lindberg does the same for regional integration. He asks: **how developed is the group's shared decision-making system?** That is different from:
- *Social community* (people feel close),
- *Security community* (they expect to settle disputes peacefully),
- *Economic union* (they trade a lot).

You can have those without shared public decisions. Lindberg wants the thing where the group **regularly makes authoritative decisions together**.

## The ten check-up items (3-4-3)
"3-4-3" is just a **memory trick** for grouping, not Lindberg's official name.

### 3 items: the LEVEL (how developed is it?)
1. **Functional scope:** how many policy areas, and how important are they?
2. **Range of decision stages:** how far through the process is it shared (spotting the problem, deciding, applying)?
3. **Relative decisiveness:** when the group decides, does it outweigh national choices on the big questions?

### 4 items: the ACTIVATORS (what keeps it running?)
4. **Demand flow:** are people actually sending requests to the group level?
5. **Resources:** money, staff, legal power, information, legitimacy.
6. **Leadership:** who actually gathers support and pushes proposals?
7. **Bargaining modalities:** do they fight for separate gains or look for joint gains?

### 3 items: the CONSEQUENCES (what happens because of it?)
8. **Penetrativeness:** whose behavior really changes?
9. **Compliance:** do people follow the decisions?
10. **Distributive consequences:** who benefits, and who pays?

**Why not just add them up?** Examples: a group can cover many areas while nations keep the final say. Another covers few areas but decides the important ones. Another has strong institutions but weak compliance. One number would hide these.

## The loop: consequences feed back
What happens in round 1 shapes round 2. Three possible directions:

| Direction | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Spillover** | Experience leads to more integration |
| **Spill-back** | Experience leads to less integration |
| **Encapsulation** | Things stay stable, pressures contained |

Warning: a disappointing result is not automatically spill-back, and a nice new plan is not automatically spillover. You must show **what changed in collective decision-making**.

## Three cases from the study guide
The guide **applies** Lindberg's ideas to these cases; Lindberg did not study them. The "spillover" labels are hypotheses.

1. **EU customs union.** Common tariff rules, but national customs officers apply them. This shows that **national officials applying binding common rules does not mean weak integration**. A recent reform created a customs authority and a data hub. It looks like possible spillover, but approval does not prove the new tools are working yet.
2. **ASEAN nursing recognition (2006, Cebu).** The region sets conditions, but the **host country's laws and regulators still license** nurses. A Philippine license alone is not enough to work abroad. Level of regional authority: limited, and specific to this issue.
3. **ASEAN haze agreement (2002).** After the 1997-1998 fires, ASEAN set up monitoring and a Haze Fund. A second roadmap came in 2023 after reviewing the first. That shows a **feedback process**, but it does not by itself prove spillover. Weather and El Nino also affect hotspot counts.

Careful with numbers: the "75% of customs duties went to the EU budget (2024)" is about where revenue goes, **not** who bears the cost.

## Why it matters, and the limits
- **Helps:** turns a vague idea into observable pieces, allows comparison across systems, issues, and time.
- **Limits:** needs a lot of data and judgment; suggestions are tentative; many examples come from Europe, so other regions need care.

## Remember this
1. Integration = an evolving **collective decision-making system**.
2. Use a **profile of ten properties**, not one score.
3. Group them: **level (3), activators (4), consequences (3)**.
4. Feedback can go **spillover, spill-back, or encapsulation**.
5. **Do not aggregate too early.**

## Don't confuse
- *Signing a treaty* is not leadership or demand flow. You need evidence of who pushed and who asked.
- *Scope vs decisiveness:* how many areas vs how much the group's choices weigh.
- *Penetrativeness vs compliance:* did behavior change vs were the rules followed.
- *Feedback vs its direction:* a review shows feedback happened; it does not show which direction.
